Best Filming Locations in Chile: A Producer's Guide to Every Region

Chile stretches 4,300 km from the driest desert on Earth to the glaciers of Patagonia. Here is a working producer's breakdown of every major region -- the look, the logistics, the permits, and the productions that got there first.

Chile has quietly become one of the most versatile filming destinations on the planet. Within a single country and a single time zone, you can shoot on Mars-like salt flats at sunrise and wrap in a European-style vineyard by sunset. The cost structure runs 35-45% below US IATSE rates, the government offers a 30% cash rebate on qualifying spend (40% outside Santiago), and the country's political stability and modern infrastructure make it one of the easiest places in South America to produce.

This guide is written for international producers, location scouts, and production managers planning a shoot in Chile. Each section covers the visual character of the region, the best window to film, how to get there from Santiago, what permits look like, and which notable productions have already used the terrain.

Central Chile

Santiago -- Modern Urban Grit and Glass

The Look
Contemporary glass towers, graffiti-lined neighborhoods, brutalist concrete, Andes backdrop
Best Time to Film
March - May, September - November (mild, clear skies)
From Santiago
You are already here
Permits
Municipality-level permits (24-72 hours). Providencia and Las Condes are most film-friendly.

Santiago offers the full spectrum of urban texture. The financial district of Sanhattan (Las Condes/Vitacura) delivers clean glass-and-steel skylines that can double for any modern city. Cross the river to Barrio Italia or Bellavista and you get narrow streets, century-old facades, and vibrant street art. The brutalist architecture of the Civic Quarter -- including La Moneda presidential palace -- reads as authoritative and institutional on camera.

The Andes mountain range is visible from almost anywhere in the city on clear days, giving wide establishing shots a dramatic natural backdrop that most cities cannot offer. The metro system is modern, Uber operates normally, and nearly all international crew gear can be sourced locally.

Notable productions: "No" (2012, Pablo Larrain), "Quantum of Solace" (2008, Bond sequence), "A Fantastic Woman" (2017, Oscar winner), and numerous Netflix and Amazon originals use Santiago as both itself and as a double for other Latin American capitals.

Central Coast

Valparaiso -- Colorful Chaos on the Pacific

The Look
Hillside color explosions, funiculars, port city grit, street murals, ocean views
Best Time to Film
October - April (warm, minimal fog)
From Santiago
90 min drive on Ruta 68. No flights needed.
Permits
Municipal permit through Valparaiso Film Commission. Plan 1-2 weeks lead time.

Valparaiso is one of the most photogenic cities in South America. UNESCO-listed, built across 42 hills, and connected by century-old funicular elevators, it offers a visual density that is almost impossible to find elsewhere. Every wall is a canvas -- murals cover entire facades, and the corrugated tin-and-concrete architecture creates a layered, textured frame at every angle.

The port area delivers industrial grit: cranes, container yards, and weathered waterfront warehouses. The cerros (hills) above offer winding streets barely wide enough for a grip truck, which means planning vehicle access carefully during pre-production. The light in Valparaiso is exceptional -- the Pacific fog burns off by mid-morning, leaving a soft, diffused quality that DPs love.

Notable productions: Scenes from "Neruda" (2016), music videos for major Latin artists, and a growing number of European commercial campaigns use Valparaiso for its unmatched color palette.

Northern Chile

Atacama Desert -- Earth's Closest Thing to Mars

The Look
Red salt flats, lunar valleys, flamingo lagoons, alien terrain, world's clearest skies
Best Time to Film
Year-round (300+ clear days). Avoid Bolivian winter rain spillover in Jan-Feb.
From Santiago
2-hour flight to Calama (CJC), then 90 min to San Pedro de Atacama
Permits
CONAF permits for national reserves. Private land access negotiated locally. Allow 2-3 weeks.

The Atacama is the driest desert on Earth, and it looks like it. The Valle de la Luna (Moon Valley) and Valle de Marte (Mars Valley) deliver terrain so alien that NASA uses it as a Mars analog for rover testing. Salt flats stretch to the horizon, geysers erupt at 4,300 meters, and the flamingo-dotted altiplanic lagoons at Salar de Atacama are surreal at any focal length.

At night, the Atacama offers the clearest skies in the world -- the reason the European Southern Observatory built its largest telescopes here. Astrophotography and sci-fi productions benefit enormously from zero light pollution.

Logistics require planning. San Pedro de Atacama is a small town with limited hotel capacity, so large crews need to book months ahead. Altitude ranges from 2,400m to 4,500m+ at the geyser fields, meaning altitude sickness protocols are not optional. Generators and water must be trucked to remote locations. Despite these constraints, the visual payoff is extraordinary.

Notable productions: "Space Odyssey" (BBC documentary), "Quantum of Solace" (desert sequences), "The Motorcycle Diaries" (2004), and multiple automotive campaigns for brands including Audi, BMW, and Land Rover.

Central Valley

Wine Country -- Vineyards with a European Feel

The Look
Rolling vineyards, colonial haciendas, golden light, Tuscan-Mediterranean atmosphere
Best Time to Film
March - April (harvest, golden vines) or October - November (green, spring bloom)
From Santiago
1-2 hours by road. Colchagua, Casablanca, and Maipo valleys are closest.
Permits
Private vineyard access through direct negotiation. Most wineries welcome production as marketing.

Chile's wine valleys offer a landscape that reads as Southern France, Tuscany, or Napa on camera -- but at a fraction of the permitting complexity and cost. The Colchagua Valley is the most visually dramatic, with rows of Carmenere and Cabernet stretching across hillsides framed by the Andes in the distance. Casablanca, closer to the coast, delivers a cooler, fog-kissed atmosphere ideal for moodier tones.

Historic hacienda estates (some dating to the 18th century) provide ready-made set pieces: clay-tile roofs, arched colonnades, barrel cellars, and manicured gardens. Many wineries actively court production companies, making location access unusually straightforward. The golden hour light during harvest season (March-April) is among the best you will find anywhere in the Southern Hemisphere.

Notable productions: Luxury brand campaigns, lifestyle editorial for international publications, and food/travel content for streaming platforms regularly film in Colchagua and Casablanca.

Southern Chile

Lakes District -- Emerald Green and Volcanic Drama

The Look
Snow-capped volcanoes over mirror lakes, temperate rainforest, German-colonial towns, lush green
Best Time to Film
December - March (dry summer). Expect rain year-round -- build weather days into schedule.
From Santiago
1.5-hour flight to Temuco (ZCO) or Puerto Montt (PMC). 2-hour drive to lake towns.
Permits
CONAF permits for national parks. Municipal permits for towns. Allow 2 weeks.

The Lakes District around Pucon, Villarrica, and Puerto Varas delivers a landscape that could double for Switzerland, New Zealand, or the Pacific Northwest. Osorno Volcano -- a nearly perfect cone -- rises above Lago Llanquihue in a composition so clean it looks computer-generated. Temperate rainforests with massive araucaria (monkey puzzle) trees provide a prehistoric canopy that works for fantasy, sci-fi, and nature documentary alike.

The region's German-colonial heritage means you will find Bavarian-style architecture, chocolate shops, and meticulously maintained lakeside towns. The infrastructure is solid: good roads, reliable hotels, and a functioning regional airport. The main production risk is weather -- southern Chile's rainfall is significant even in summer, so smart scheduling builds in buffer days.

Notable productions: "The Revenant" considered the region during pre-production, nature documentaries for National Geographic and BBC Earth regularly film here, and automotive brands use the volcano-and-lake backdrops for SUV and adventure vehicle campaigns.

Far South

Patagonia -- Glaciers, Granite, and the End of the World

The Look
Granite towers, glaciers, turquoise lakes, endless steppe, extreme-weather drama
Best Time to Film
November - March (summer, 16+ hours of daylight). Wind is constant -- plan accordingly.
From Santiago
3.5-hour flight to Punta Arenas (PUQ), then 4-hour drive to Torres del Paine.
Permits
CONAF national park permit required (strict). Submit 30+ days in advance. Drone permits separate and harder to obtain.

Torres del Paine is the crown jewel. The three granite towers -- 2,500 meters of vertical rock rising from an ancient glacial valley -- are among the most photographed natural landmarks in the world. Grey Glacier calves icebergs into a turquoise lake. The Patagonian steppe stretches flat and infinite, broken only by guanaco herds and the occasional gaucho on horseback.

Patagonia demands respect from a production standpoint. Wind speeds regularly hit 80-120 km/h, which means every piece of grip equipment needs to be anchored or it will become a projectile. The nearest full-service city is Punta Arenas (population 130,000), so heavy equipment, generators, and specialized gear must be trucked or flown in. Accommodation near Torres del Paine is limited and books out months in advance during summer.

The payoff is visual material that cannot be replicated anywhere else on Earth. The scale is humbling, the light changes minute by minute, and the 16+ hours of summer daylight give you an extended shooting window that compensates for the logistical overhead.

Notable productions: "Planet Earth" and "Our Planet" (BBC/Netflix glacier sequences), "180 Degrees South" (documentary), Patagonia brand campaigns, and high-end automotive commercials for Porsche, Mercedes, and Toyota.

Cordillera

The Andes Mountains -- Snow, Altitude, and Vertical Scale

The Look
Snow-covered peaks, high-altitude plateaus, ski resorts, mountain passes, dramatic verticality
Best Time to Film
June - September (snow), December - March (clear alpine). Year-round above 3,500m.
From Santiago
Ski resorts: 90 min drive. Extreme altitude locations: 3-5 hours by road.
Permits
Ski resort permits through resort management. Backcountry requires CONAF and sometimes military coordination near borders.

The Andes run the entire length of Chile, but the most accessible high-altitude filming is within two hours of Santiago. Ski resorts like Valle Nevado (3,000m), Portillo (2,880m), and Farellones offer ready-made mountain infrastructure -- chairlifts for camera platforms, groomed terrain, lodging, and power. Portillo's iconic yellow hotel against a frozen Laguna del Inca has become a classic establishing shot.

For more extreme altitude work, the Paso Los Libertadores border crossing road climbs to nearly 3,200m and offers stark, windswept mountain passes. Further north, the altiplano above 4,000m delivers a completely different Andes character -- sparse, volcanic, and otherworldly.

Altitude is the primary production consideration. Any location above 3,000m requires acclimatization time for crew, and medical oxygen should be on set. Cold-weather battery management and condensation protocols for lenses are non-negotiable above the snowline.

Notable productions: "Alive" (1993, Andes plane crash), ski and snowboard films for Red Bull and The North Face, automotive winter testing footage, and high-fashion editorial shoots at Portillo.

Pacific Coast

The Chilean Coast -- Cliffs, Surf, and Endless Pacific

The Look
Dramatic sea cliffs, black sand beaches, fishing villages, powerful surf, rugged Pacific coastline
Best Time to Film
October - April (warmer, calmer seas). Winter storms for dramatic ocean footage.
From Santiago
Central coast: 90 min. Northern coast (Pan de Azucar): fly + drive. Southern coast (Chiloe): fly to PMC + ferry.
Permits
Beach access generally unrestricted. Port areas require Capitania de Puerto authorization. Marine filming needs Navy coordination.

Chile's 6,400 km coastline is one of the longest and most visually varied on the planet. The central coast near Zapallar and Cachagua offers Mediterranean-feeling coves with clear water and rocky headlands. Move south to the Maule coast and you get wild, deserted black-sand beaches with crashing Pacific surf. The Isla Negra stretch -- where Pablo Neruda built his famous house -- delivers a moody, literary atmosphere with wave-battered rocks and weathered timber homes.

Further north, Pan de Azucar National Park combines desert cliffs dropping into the Pacific with Humboldt penguin colonies -- a combination that exists nowhere else. In the far south, the island of Chiloe offers wooden churches (UNESCO), stilt houses over water, and a mystical fog-and-rain atmosphere that reads as Celtic or Scandinavian on camera.

Coastal logistics are generally straightforward. Most of the central and northern coast is accessible by paved road. Southern coastal locations (especially Chiloe and the fjords) may require ferry crossings that need to be booked in advance for production vehicles.

Notable productions: Surf documentaries, "Neruda" (coastal sequences at Isla Negra), automotive coastal road-trip campaigns, and European fashion shoots that use Chilean beaches in January-February when European beaches are frozen.

A Quick Note on Filming Permits in Chile

Chile does not have a single national film permit. Permits are managed at the municipal level for urban locations and through CONAF (Corporacion Nacional Forestal) for national parks and protected areas. The process is generally straightforward compared to other Latin American countries, but lead times vary:

  • Santiago municipalities: 3-7 business days
  • CONAF national parks: 15-30 days minimum
  • Drone permits (DGAC): 10-20 business days, separate from location permits
  • Marine/naval areas: Coordinate through Capitania de Puerto, allow 3-4 weeks
  • Military zones (border areas, some Andes): Requires coordination through the production service company

Having a local production service company handle permits is not just recommended -- it is practically necessary. Permit applications are in Spanish, relationships with municipal contacts matter, and fee structures vary by municipality. A good local producer will also know which locations technically require permits but are routinely accessed without issue, and which locations have hidden restrictions that can shut down a shoot day.

Why Chile Over Other Destinations

The argument for Chile as a filming destination comes down to three factors that are hard to match anywhere else. First, geographic compression: no other country on Earth offers desert, mountains, glaciers, vineyards, coast, and modern cities within a single time zone and a 3-hour flight radius. This means a two-week shoot can cover terrain that would normally require three or four countries.

Second, cost efficiency. Chilean crew rates run 35-45% below US union scale, with world-class talent -- particularly in cinematography, grip, and electrical departments that have been trained on major international productions. The 30% cash rebate through the IFI Audiovisual program (40% for spend outside Santiago) further compresses budgets.

Third, reliability. Chile is the most politically and economically stable country in South America. The peso is freely convertible, international banking works normally, equipment imports clear customs in days not weeks, and corruption at the permit level is essentially nonexistent. When you schedule a shoot day, it happens.
